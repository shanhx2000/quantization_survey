Abstract
Hyper-Dimensional Computing (HDC) is a promising braininspired learning framework designed for efficient, hardwarefriendly computation. By utilizing highly parallel operations, HDC
encodes raw data into a hyper-dimensional space, facilitating efficient training and inference processes. However, the high precision required for representing high-dimensional vectors presents
challenges for implementing HDC on resource-constrained edge
devices, mainly due to the significant computational cost of performing multiplication for cosine similarity calculations. On the
other hand, binary HDC offers lower costs but sacrifices accuracy.
This paper addresses these challenges by focusing on data quantization, a hardware-efficient compression technique that incorporates sparsity to effectively balance cost and accuracy on embedded
FPGA. Unlike existing methods that employ the same quantization scheme for all dimensions, we propose a novel solution that
applies different numerical data types to different dimensions of
data representations. This approach is motivated by two factors:

Introduction

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable advancements across a wide range of applications, including image recognition, target tracking, and text generation. However, the current
DNN models often pose significant challenges for on-device inference due to their substantial model sizes, requiring gigabytes (GBs)
of storage, and the high computational demands of performing
inference computations, which may reach 102 Giga Floating Point
Operations (GFLOPs) [40, 41].
In light of these challenges, brain-inspired computing has
emerged as a promising alternative for deploying DNNs in scenarios that require high energy efficiency and low latency [29, 30, 37,
39, 58]. One notable computational paradigm within brain-inspired
computing is Hyper-Dimensional Computing (HDC) [21, 23], which
draws inspiration from the distributed representations found in
neuronal circuits of the human brain. HDC involves mapping and
encoding raw data into a hyper-dimensional space with a dimensionality ≥ 1, 000, using well-designed basic operations that exhibit
a high degree of parallelism. This encoding process ensures the retention of numerous features present in the original data. Once the
data is encoded, HDC constructs an associative memory through
single-shot training. Inference is then performed by conducting
an associative search based on cosine similarity. Notably, hyperdimensional computing also supports model binarization and associative search based on Hamming Distance, making it highly
competitive in edge computing scenarios [28, 31, 34–36, 46, 62].
As a result, HDC has found wide applications in various domains,
including time series processing [9, 42], speech recognition [11],
anomaly detection [57], activity recognition [24], text classification [38, 45], and bio-signal recognition [48, 64].
To fully leverage the advantages of HDC models, several challenges must be addressed: (i) the computational overhead of the
associative search module in HDC, which relies on multiplication
operations that are generally more resource-intensive than addition
and logic operations. This computational overhead in relation to
dimensionality and precision, limits scalability and hinders deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. Our evaluation on an
embedded FPGA with a single-sample inference shows that, on
average, the computational cost of the associative search module
is 3.05× higher than that of the encoding module when each HDC
model is configured with a dimension of 1, 000 and quantized to
INT8 (as presented in Table 1). Thus, reducing the computational
overhead of the associative search module is a primary focus for
improving efficiency. (ii) the limitations of existing compression
techniques, as most current methods adopt strategies from DNNs,
such as conventional pruning [18, 43] and quantization [10, 17],
which often achieve limited improvements in accuracy and speed;
(iii) the ineffective handling of dimension removal and redundancy

dimensionality-agnostic approach ignores both intra-dimension
and inter-dimension redundancies, resulting in either low compression rates (to preserve accuracy) or compromised accuracy (at
higher compression rates). Moreover, these methods lack support
for efficient hardware implementation on edge devices, even though
HDC models are intrinsically suitable for acceleration on embedded
FPGAs due to their natural algorithm-level parallelism. Therefore,
it is crucial to improve the hardware efficiency of these computations while capitalizing on the optimization potential offered by
dimensional redundancies.
In this paper, we propose FATE, a hardware-efficient dimensionadaptive quantization framework, together with dimensional sparsity, for efficient yet accurate HDC inference acceleration on embedded FPGA platforms. FATE addresses the limitations of using a
fixed-length quantization by implementing a fine-grained quantization scheme that applies different data types to different dimensions.
This method is motivated by two key factors: (1) the varying importance of dimensions within hypervectors, and (2) the potential
for better utilization of heterogeneous FPGA hardware resources.
FATE incorporates in-depth analysis and ordering of dimensions
based on their importance. By identifying and considering the varying significance of each dimension, we can assign appropriate data
types that best preserve accuracy for the respective dimensions.
This fine-grained compression approach combines the benefits of
integrated sparsity and mixed quantization, allowing for a more
efficient representation of HDC models.
To fully exploit the capabilities of FPGA resources, we propose
an efficient mixed bit-width HDC inference architecture. This architecture is designed to accommodate the heterogeneous quantization
requirements of different dimensions, leveraging the flexibility of
FPGA hardware to efficiently execute HDC operations. In addition,
we develop a workload-aware mixed quantization scaling strategy,
which takes into account the specific characteristics of the target
workload. This strategy optimizes the quantization scaling factors
for different dimensions based on the workload requirements. By
tailoring quantization to workload characteristics, we improve inference accuracy and maximize hardware resource utilization.

2.2

HDC Model Compression

To optimize the energy consumption and performance of HDC, various model compression techniques have been proposed, including
dimensionality reduction and quantization. However, these existing
techniques have certain limitations: CompHD [43] relies on simple
binary orthogonal vectors for dot product to achieve dimensionality
reduction, but this approach results in a significant accuracy loss.
SparseHD [18] employs a dimensional importance discriminant for
coarse-grained dimensionality reduction but fails to fully exploit the
variations in dimensional importance, resulting in suboptimal compression. QuantHD [10] uses ternary quantization to preserve more
information, yet the redundancy in high-dimensional representations can obscure accuracy loss from quantization. Additionally,
these methods often rely on iterative compression and fine-tuning
to achieve satisfactory performance.
On the other hand, novel quantization strategies, such as
FACH [17, 44, 53], optimize computations by clustering values
and converting part of the similarity computation into addition,
thereby reducing multiplication overhead. Notably, these quantization strategies function independently of global model compression,
making them orthogonal to our approach. This distinction is further
demonstrated in Section 5.

2.3

Analysis and Motivation

The evaluation in Table 1 highlights the challenge of balancing
hardware cost and accuracy with fixed data bit-widths. Additionally, Figure 3 shows that both INT8 HDC and binary HDC models
struggle to fully utilize the available resources1 . Specifically, INT8
HDC occupies almost all the DSPs but less than half of the LUTs,
while binary HDC uses few DSPs but slightly more LUTs.
In light of the above challenges, we identify two key opportunities that can serve as the foundation for constructing a hardwarefriendly adaptive quantization framework for HDCs:
• Intra-Hypervector Adaptivity: By recognizing the varying importance of values within each hypervector, we can
selectively apply different data types to different segments.
This intra-hypervector adaptivity allows us to maintain high
accuracy in critical regions while using lower precision for

• Inter-Dimensionality Adaptivity: By analyzing the specific distribution of each dimension, we can tailor the quantization scheme to suit the characteristics of different dimensions. This adaptivity enables aligned, finer-grained compression performance for each dimension, leading to more

Capitalizing on the simplicity and high parallelism of elementwise operations in HDCs, we believe fine-grained mixed compression is feasible and can lead to superior performance. In this paper,
we conduct an in-depth analysis of the dimensional importance of
hypervectors in HDC. We exploit this analysis to propose a finegrained data type mixed compression framework that unifies and
integrates dimension pruning and quantization. Moreover, we introduce a workload-aware data type mixed scaling strategy to take
advantage of the fine-grained nature of our approach. Finally, we
present a flexible and efficient hardware-software co-design for
HDC compression.

3

FATE Framework

In this section, we present FATE, a novel approach that combines
different numeric data type quantization schemes for FPGA implementations of HDC inference models. Firstly, we analyze the
impact of dimension on the associative search process in the AM.
Next, we present a flexible framework that adapts to the dimension distribution of hypervectors by selecting suitable numeric data
types. As a result, in our FATE approach, we exploit diverse data
types during HDC model quantization, achieving two advantages:
(1) better FPGA resource allocation, and (2) negligible accuracy loss.

3.1

Algorithm Overview

As shown in Figure 4, FATE receives two inputs: a well-trained HDC
model and compression requirement. The input model consists of
IM, CIM, and AM, while the compression requirement is mainly
about the rate of each kind of bit-width that the users wish to use.
FATE includes three modules: dimensional analysis module,
mixed bit-width alignment module, and compression module. The
dimensional analysis module measures the difference between the
dimensions of the HDC model to be compressed. The mixed bitwidth alignment module aligns the model’s dimensions with the
compression requirement. The compression module compresses
the HDC module according to the alignment result.

the dimensions will be represented using INT8, the middle 30% will
be represented using INT4, and the last 20% of the dimensions will
be pruned. When compression requirements include dimensionality

of the list should be deleted and send the information to subsequent
modules as instructions for compression.

According to the relative order of the original dimensions, it is very

the dimensions in AM: let each bit-width occur as equally as possible in different segments, given the known dimensionality of the
segments. In this way, resource scheduling can be circumvented as
much as possible, and workloads become stable.
Consider an HDC model with 1, 000 dimensions and the compression requirements are 50% INT8 values and 50% INT4 values.
Additionally, the hardware resources only support the computation
of a 100-dimensional vector multiplication per clock cycle. Thus,
we need to divide the class vector into 10 segments, each containing
a 100-dimensional vector, and compute them sequentially. Without
rearrangement, one segmented vector might have 80 INT8 elements
and 20 INT4 elements, while another segmented vector might have
10 INT8 elements and 90 INT4 elements. This situation leads to an
unbalanced workload and requires complex resource scheduling. In
contrast, after rearrangement, each segmented vector will have 50
INT8 and 50 INT4 elements. This balanced distribution greatly simplifies the control logic and resource allocation, reducing workload
variation and enabling more efficient computation.

Efficient Online Inference

In the FATE framework, the online inference process, as depicted
in Figure 4, is straightforward and clear, even with the presence
of mixed bit-widths. When a sample is input into the model, it is
first mapped and encoded by IM and CIM. As both IM and CIM
have been rearranged, the sample hypervector aligns with the class
hypervectors in AM. This alignment ensures a relatively stable
workload during the iterative computation of dividing and processing the hypervectors in segments. Consequently, computational
resources can be fully utilized. Additionally, the use of different bitwidth quantization introduces distinct scaling in each dimension
during element-wise multiplication. As a result, the multiplication
result undergoes appropriate shifting to ensure correctness. By
leveraging these techniques, including rearrangement, workload
stabilization, and proper scaling, the FATE framework achieves
an efficient and effective online inference process. This approach
allows for smooth handling of mixed bit-widths, enabling accurate
and quick computations during inference.

3.6

Adjustment Mechanism

To enhance performance, we introduce an adaptive adjustment
mechanism aimed at improving the accuracy of the compressed
model through iterative rounds of compression and updating. The
process involves the following steps: (i) Compression and Storage:
Initially, the model is compressed as per the specified criteria, and
an uncompressed copy is retained; (ii) Inference and Update: The
compressed model is utilized for inference on the training set. Misclassified samples are identified, and the original uncompressed
model is updated using the retraining approach outlined in the
preceding section; (iii) Subsequent Rounds: In the subsequent iteration, the previously updated uncompressed model is subjected to
compression. This iteration is repeated for several rounds.

4.1

Architecture Overview

Based on the FATE framework, we designed the architecture, providing hardware support for efficient inference, as depicted in Figure 6.
The FATE architecture consists of two primary components: encoding and associative search. The encoding part is responsible for
transforming the input samples into the high-dimensional space
through projection. This process involves encoding the samples as
hypervectors for further processing. On the other hand, the associative search part is responsible for searching the AM to find the most
similar class hypervectors corresponding to the encoded hypervectors. This search operation is crucial for accurately classifying the
input samples.

4.2

Encoding Design

As shown in Figure 6, the encoding design comprises three key
components: base hypervector memory, XOR accumulation array,
and segment buffer. Base Hypervector Memory serves as storage
for the base hypervectors extracted from IM and CIM. For each
dimensional feature data of the input sample, the corresponding
feature base hypervector and feature value base hypervector in
memory will be activated and are activated and read out to supply
the XOR Accumulation Array for computation. The XOR Accumulation Array calculates the element-wise XOR result of the two base
hypervectors obtained from memory. It then performs an elementwise addition with the query hypervector, which is initialized as a
zero hypervector. The Segment Buffer stores encoded results from
the accumulation array. High-dimensional data may require multiple steps for inference in the associative search part. Therefore,
the encoded result is divided into smaller query vectors (e.g., 100
dimensions). These vectors are sent to the associative search part
one by one, and the inference process is completed after several
rounds.

1275

4.3

Associative Search Design

In Figure 7, the associative search architecture is depicted with
three main designs: compressed class hypervector storage, mixed
bit-width multiplier array, and adder tree.
In this part, the goal is to compute the similarity between each
query hypervector and all class hypervectors in parallel. This is
achieved by performing element-wise multiplication for the similarity computation, taking into account the constraints on parallelism.
Embedded FPGA devices have a limited number of DSPs, typically
not exceeding 1,000, and a few dozen classes are usually present.
This results in a relatively small number of high bit-width dimensions involved in the computation, emphasizing the significance
of the segmented cache in the encoding part to ensure efficient
processing and storage management during computation.
The left side of Figure 7 shows the storage of compressed class
hypervectors. The hypervectors are stored in RAM, and when the
query vector arrives, multiple class vectors are computed in parallel
with the query vector at the same time to fully utilize the parallel
processing capabilities of HDC. Although the mixed bit-width data
storage introduces some addressing overhead, it is significantly
smaller than the saved multiplication overhead, making it a worthwhile trade-off for efficient processing.
The mixed bit-width multiplier array is the core of our design.
It processes the class hypervector and query hypervector after
addressing. To fully utilize the available resources, we employ different implementations for various bit-width dimensions. For INT8,
we utilize DSP resources to implement the multiplier. For lower
bit-widths like INT4 and ternary, we leverage LUT implementation. For binary calculations, a logic circuit decides whether the
query hypervector value should be set to 0 or remain unchanged.
While the implementation of low bit-widths with LUTs is used, the
number of LUTs employed varies depending on the bit-width. This
allows us to control the resource utilization effectively by adjusting compression requirements based on the available computing
resources.
The multiplication results of different bit-width will be shifted
and aligned to ensure correctness. Specifically, the INT4 results are
shifted 4 bits to the left, the ternary results are shifted 6 bits to the
left, and the binary results are shifted 7 bits to the left. For example,
if a result is 162, which is multiplied by 54 (from the query vector)

Pipeline Design

To efficiently utilize computational resources and reduce inference
latency, we design an optimized data flow. Figure 8(a) illustrates
the computational flow from the original sample feature data to
the encoded hypervector. Due to resource limitations, the encoded
hypervector is divided into 𝐷/𝑑 segments in the segment buffer,
where 𝐷 is the dimension of the hypervector, and 𝑑 is the maximum dimension for a single parallel computation in the associative
search.
In the associative search part, each round computes a segmented
vector of length 𝑑, denoted as 𝑄𝑖 . The computation of 𝑄𝑖 involves
three main steps: ❶ mixed bit-width element-wise multiplication,
❷ shift adjustment, and ❸ tree accumulation.
In Figure 8(b), we depict an efficient execution flow. To simplify
control, we ensure uniform pipeline levels for different bit-width
multipliers through data registration, typically employing a 3-level
pipeline in practice. Once 𝑄𝑖 enters the associative search module,
all multiplication units output their results after three cycles. The
addition tree module uses a 𝑙𝑜𝑔(𝑑)-level pipeline structure to efficiently perform the summation on the multiplication result vector.
The shift operation is performed by concatenating the multiplication result with zero bits and feeding them into the addition tree,
ensuring seamless and efficient computation of 𝑄𝑖 .

CARDIO datasets. This is because the range-based method does not
adequately describe the importance of dimensions: it only describes
the maximum discriminative power of a dimension to distinguish
two classes, but does not describe the sum of the discriminative
power of a dimension for any two dimensions, and thus there is
some instability in this method. Our method, on the other hand,
has higher stability and always outperforms the random method.

Due to the unique computational model of HDC, such as high
dimensionality and high parallelism, existing compression methods
for DNNs do not fit HDC, and even the latest hybrid precision neural
network inference accelerator[50] can not be effectively migrated
to HDC models. Therefore, there is a lack of a powerful and stable
compression method to make HDC have better performance.

6.2

Binary HDC Design

Binary HDCs are efficient but may sacrifice accuracy compared to
floating-point or INT8 HDCs. To improve accuracy, various works
explore enhancing the binarized HDC framework [8]. For instance,
SearcHD [19] uses multiple binary hypervectors per class to store
more information. BRIC [15] applies random projection methods
for better encoding. BinHD [13] introduces a counter-based update
strategy for finer-grained class hypervector adjustments. However,
these optimization methods often increase computational complexity. In contrast, the proposed FATE method reduces computational
overhead without adding framework complexity. Its scalability allows it to be combined with existing optimizations for both accuracy
and performance.

6.3

HDC Accelerators

In addition to the framework optimization of binary HDC, there
are many efforts to accelerate HDC. Some schemes are based on
emerging in-store computing to accelerate HDC. The work in [16]
designed three popular digital, resistive, and analog associative
memories to accelerate Hamming distance similarity during associative search. HD nanosystem in [60] uses monolithic 3D integration of carbon nanotube field-effect transistors and resistive
RAM to accelerate binary HDC for text classification. However,

1280

these methods only support Hamming distance similarity between
binarized hypervectors.
Other acceleration solutions are based on FPGAs and low-power
architectures. LookHD [20] exploits computation reuse to memorize
the encoding stage and multiple class hypervectors compression
for lower inference latency and storage cost. F5-HD [52] proposes
a template-based framework that can generate FPGA-based synthesizable HDC architectures and a novel hardware-friendly encoding approach that enhances resource utilization. Also, it provides
the flexibility of customized accuracy by supporting different data
types.
In summary, these existing acceleration frameworks, which are
all capable of using only a single numerical data format in a single
inference, have room for optimization in terms of resource utilization and efficiency. Our approach can exactly further explore
the optimization possibilities and obtain better performance and
flexibility.

7

Conclusion

In this paper, we aim to build hyper-dimensional computing frameworks that can flexibly adapt to the requirements of different edge
computing scenarios. We perform dimensional importance analysis on hyperdimensional vectors and introduce mixed bit-width
compression to design a resource-aware HDC architecture called
FATE, an efficient and flexible compression for hyperdimensional
computing models. Our FATE is designed with a stable and excellent dimensional importance measure and corresponding mixed
bit-width compression strategy, and further reduces energy/latency
and improves resource utilization through resource-aware architectural design. Experiments show that FATE can deliver satisfactory
performance gain with trivial accuracy loss.
In future work, we plan to enhance FATE by incorporating an
automated optimization loop that searches for the optimal compression parameters. This approach would allow FATE to adjust
the compression rate dynamically using a binary search strategy,
ensuring that the accuracy remains within the specified threshold
without requiring manual input from users.
